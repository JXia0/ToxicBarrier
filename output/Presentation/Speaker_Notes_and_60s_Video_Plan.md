## 英文讲稿

### Slide 1 — Two game projects

Today I’m presenting two of my game projects. ToxicBarrier is a digital survival game about protecting a tower. Vision Zero is a competitive board game about planning safer streets. I’ll explain the main decisions in each game, with a short gameplay video for ToxicBarrier.

### Slide 2 — ToxicBarrier: the core loop

In ToxicBarrier, the player and the central tower are on the same side. The tower attacks automatically, while the player moves around the map, collects materials and draws enemies into its range. The tower starts at level zero and can reach level three. Upgrades improve it and open access to more of the map. The main decision is when to explore and when to return. Staying close is safer, but collecting resources requires leaving that protection. To win, the player must fully upgrade the tower and clear the waves and remaining enemies in all three regions.

### Slide 3 — ToxicBarrier: movement and feedback

I’ve been refining the decisions around movement. Buildings now give players routes around enemies. If a building blocks an enemy’s view, it searches the last place it saw the player before returning to the tower. This lets positioning change a fight. The orange circle shown here warns the player before a boss attack lands. There are also limited repair supplies in the outer region. Bringing one back can save the tower, but the trip means spending time away from it. The next video shows these decisions in action.

### Slide 5 — Vision Zero: how the game works

Vision Zero moves from real-time action to planning around a table. It is a competitive game for two to four players, played over five rounds. Each player leads a city-planning team with three employees. Players send them to departments on this shared board to obtain funding, choose projects, gain policies or invest in infrastructure. Then they draft safety dice and place them on projects. A limited supply means a useful die may disappear before your next turn. Projects earn Safety Points when their requirements are complete. The player with the highest final score wins. The board and component designs are prepared for a physical prototype.

### Slide 6 — Vision Zero: a project example

Here is a simple example. The Elementary Student project needs one crosswalk and one sidewalk, and it is worth three Safety Points. A player drafts matching symbols to complete it. But the choice sits inside a larger plan. Do I take the die that finishes this project now, or build toward another project? How much funding should I keep for emergencies? The Heavy Rain and Fog event on the right adds two emergency dice to the pool. That makes saving money more useful, because many emergency penalties can be mitigated with Coins. Permanent infrastructure offers another option: a reusable safety symbol for future projects. I want the transport theme to affect decisions through the rules, so different travelers and changing conditions matter during play.

### Slide 7 — Closing

These projects explore decisions at different speeds. ToxicBarrier asks players to react through movement and choose when to leave the tower. Vision Zero asks them to plan across rounds with limited resources. My focus across both is to give players clear choices and visible consequences. Thank you.
